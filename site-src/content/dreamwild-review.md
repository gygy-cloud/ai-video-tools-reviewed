## What DreamWild Actually Is

You type one idea; DreamWild writes the script, generates scenes, adds narration and captions, and returns a vertical video capped at **60 seconds**, which it can upload to YouTube for you. The style library is the differentiator, aimed at faceless Shorts: Pixar-look, Ghibli-look, Kurzgesagt-look, stick figure, story-over-gameplay, "Zack D. Films." Beside it sit an AI image tool, a YouTube script writer, thumbnails, channel automation and an MCP integration for Claude and ChatGPT. That names the audience: people running a faceless Shorts channel, not agencies making brand films.

## Clause 5.9: The Cap Nobody Quotes

> The total Commission on a Referred Customer never exceeds what their payments have earned us since their First Payment: the Net Revenue from them, less the payment-processing fees on those payments and the supplier cost of the videos they make.

Three details make this more than boilerplate.

**The arithmetic is real.** Net revenue is what the customer paid, after discounts, excluding tax, less refunds and chargebacks — and clause 5.3 says DreamWild does **not** deduct processing fees from it. So your 35% is computed on a base that ignores card fees; 5.9 then subtracts them, "at the highest standard rate our payment processor charges," plus the supplier cost of every video that customer generated. Most programs disclose neither.

**The cap is per customer, not per month.** A customer paying $89 for two years generates $2,136 of net revenue; the cap is that total minus fees minus the cost of the ~1,080 Shorts they made. It bites when cumulative video production outruns cumulative payments, not on renewal one.

**The cost figure is partly internal.** Clause 5.9(c) counts "the supplier cost we recorded for each of their videos and, where we could not record a cost, a standard estimate based on the credits the video used." Neither the formula nor the cost per video is published. The number that limits your commission is calculated by the counterparty from inputs you cannot audit.

What you *can* compute is the threshold at which the cap starts to matter, using the revenue-per-Short figures above and an assumed 2.9% + $0.30 processing rate (5.9(a) names no processor):

| Plan | Net revenue per 30-second Short | Cap bites above roughly this all-in cost per Short |
|---|---|---|
| **Starter** | $4.83 | **~$4.64** |
| **Creator** | $1.98 | **~$1.91** |
| **Pro** | $1.99 | **~$1.93** |
| **Max** | $2.00 | **~$1.94** |

Read that as a warning about your best customers. The plan that is worst value for the subscriber — Starter, at 250 credits per Short — is the *safest* for the affiliate, because it generates $4.83 of revenue per video the company must pay to render. The high-volume plans, which produce the largest lifetime commissions, generate about **$2 of revenue per video**. Once DreamWild's all-in cost per Short crosses roughly $1.90 there, 5.9 starts trimming commission — on exactly the customers you were counting on.

The mitigating text is fairer than most cap clauses: reductions come from "the most recent Commission on that customer first, and restore[d] as their later payments cover it," and 6.4 limits refund clawbacks to the commission on the reversed amount. But 6.3 is the clause to read twice: if your balance cannot cover a clawback within 90 days, **DreamWild may invoice you for the shortfall, payable in 30 days**. Most programs simply zero the balance. This one can send a bill.

## "Lifetime" With a Heartbeat Clause

Every program here advertises lifetime commission, and this site has argued the term is table stakes rather than a differentiator. DreamWild is the first reviewed here that defines it as an ongoing obligation:

> You stay an Active Creator by having at least one Genuine Click on your Link in every 12 months.

Miss that and 13.2(b) applies: DreamWild may end the agreement on 30 days' notice, which lapses only if a tracked click lands first. A 12-month window is generous and one click resets it, so this reads as anti-dormancy rather than a trap. But residual income that requires you to keep publishing is not the same asset as income that pays while you sleep, and a post ranking for two years without a new click is exactly the profile this targets. What survives termination is genuinely good: under 13.4, if the agreement ends for inactivity or company-side reasons, customers whose first payment predates the end date keep earning while they keep paying.

## Auditing the $65,015 Calculator

The Creator Program page carries an earnings widget. Set it to five paying Creator customers a month for one year and it shows **US$65,015 by month 36**, against **US$16,020** for "a typical program: 25% for 12 months." The disclaimer is more honest than most: "An illustration, not a promise of earnings. Assumes one year of posting and every subscriber staying subscribed."

I rebuilt the model from the published inputs — 60 Creator customers at $89, 35% until ten clear the hold and 40% after, all 36 months — and got **$65,126**, within 0.2% of the widget. Good arithmetic, fatal assumption: the only way to reproduce it is to assume **literally zero churn**.

| Monthly churn | 36-month commission |

| 3% | **$41,572** |

At 5% monthly churn — roughly a 20-month average customer life, unremarkable for an $89 creator tool — the widget overstates by half. Clause 5.6 says as much in legal language: calculator figures are "illustrations, not forecasts or promises." Quote the arithmetic, not the headline, and state the assumption.

## What You May and May Not Publish

Clause 8.1 is why a written review is viable at all: "Organic promotion is welcome: tutorials, reviews, demos, comparisons, newsletters, courses, communities and your own channels, including honest opinions, good and bad." That contrasts directly with HeyGen, which permits original video only and rules out SEO and blog-only promotion — the reason that program does not appear on this site.

Three prohibitions bind a review site specifically. **8.2(h)** bans saying anyone "will or could earn money, get monetised, or gain views, subscribers or followers," or stating anyone's earnings, without written permission — which is why the calculator above is framed as arithmetic on the vendor's inputs. **8.2(i)** bans describing DreamWild as free or discounted "unless our current published offer says so." **8.2(s)** bans saying you use DreamWild when you do not, "or describ[ing] an experience of it that you have not genuinely had" — making the line between a documentation review and a hands-on test contractual, not stylistic.

One friction point: the program is written for people who **post videos**. Onboarding asks you to "post a video with #dreamwild and your link in bio," and the terms do not say what a hashtag requirement means for a written page. If your distribution is text-first, ask support@dreamwild.ai in writing first. Joining also requires a DreamWild account in good standing (cl. 3.1) — not a standalone signup like Rewardful or Tolt.

## FAQ

**How much does the DreamWild affiliate program pay?**
35% of net revenue on every plan payment and extra-credit purchase, rising to 40% once ten referred customers have each made a first payment that cleared its 30-day hold. Clause 5.9 caps total commission on any one customer at what their payments earned DreamWild, minus processing fees and the supplier cost of their videos.

**Is it really lifetime?**
The commission period runs for the life of the customer, but you must stay an Active Creator — at least one genuine tracked click every 12 months. After 12 months without one, DreamWild may end the agreement on 30 days' notice.

**When do I get paid?**
Monthly, once your available balance reaches $10, by PayPal, Wise or USDT at 1:1. Each payment is held 30 days first; smaller balances roll over and network fees are deducted.

**Can I run ads?**
Not without written approval. 8.2(a) bans paid advertising and paid traffic to the link or the site; 8.2(b) bans brand bidding. Organic reviews and comparisons are allowed under 8.1.
