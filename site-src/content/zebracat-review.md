## What Is Zebracat?

Zebracat is a browser-based AI video generator aimed at marketers and content teams rather than filmmakers. You give it text, a link, or a rough idea; it returns a finished vertical or square video with voiceover, captions, and b-roll assembled for you.

The architectural choice that matters: Zebracat does not train its own video model. It sits on top of several — the pricing page names **Seedance 2.5, Kling 3.0, Veo 3.1, and Gemini Omni**, and states that new models are added as they ship. For each scene, the platform picks a model rather than making you choose.

That is a genuine differentiator, and also the source of its main weakness. You are paying a markup for orchestration. If you already know exactly which model you want and how to prompt it, a raw credit-based studio will usually cost less per render.

The vendor states **50,000+ creators** and **2 million+ videos made**. Those are self-reported marketing figures, not audited numbers — treat them as directional.

**Two things the pricing page does not tell you clearly:**

**First, the credit number is not a comparable unit.** Cat Mode gives you 15 videos and 350 credits. Super Cat gives you 40 videos and 1,400 credits — exactly 4× on both. But a render that uses Kling 3.0 or Veo 3.1 costs more credits than one using a lighter model, and Zebracat does not publish a per-model credit table on its pricing page. So "350 credits" tells you nothing until you know which models you will actually route through. Budget by video count, then watch your credit burn for the first month.

**Second, the full tier comparison only appears after signup.** The public pricing page at zebracat.ai/pricing shows a single entry price of $39/mo. The three-column comparison with video counts and credits appears inside the app, after you create an account. If you are comparing tools before committing an email address, that is friction — and it is why most reviews of this product quote numbers they could only have seen from inside a logged-in session.

There is also a promotional banner offering voiceovers, AI images and scripts at zero credits for subscribers, described as locked in "for life." Treat that as a marketing claim with no stated expiry or contractual terms behind it. It is a reason to be pleased if it holds, not a reason to buy.

## What Zebracat Actually Does Well

**Model routing is real value, not marketing.** Most AI video tools are wrappers around one model. If Kling handles motion better but Veo handles lighting better, a single-model tool forces you to accept the tradeoff. Zebracat's pitch is that it decides per scene. For someone producing 15–40 short videos a month, not having to think about that is worth actual money.

**Output volume is high for the price.** Unlimited videos at $119/month billed yearly is aggressive. Compare that to credit-based studios where a single 5-second 720p render can cost 150+ credits.

**The workflow is end-to-end.** The plans bundle video ideas, script and voiceover generation, and posting. That collapses what is otherwise four tools — idea research, scripting, generation, scheduling — into one bill. Whether the posting features are good enough to replace a dedicated scheduler is a separate question, but the intent is right.

**The free tier is a real free tier.** No card, and it lets you run the complete workflow rather than watermarked 5-second samples. That is enough to judge output quality before paying.

## Where Zebracat Falls Short

**You are renting someone else's models.** If Seedance or Kling change their pricing or licensing, Zebracat's costs change and so does your output. You have no lever over that.

**The credit system is opaque at the tier level.** As above, no published per-model credit costs. You cannot compute cost-per-video before subscribing.

**Annual prepay is where the discount lives.** The 40% saving requires paying for twelve months up front. If you produce video in bursts — a launch month, then two quiet months — you are paying for idle capacity.

**No public feature-by-feature comparison.** The pricing page's "see all features" anchor leads into a comparison that is thin on the public site. For a tool charging $199/month at the top tier, the pre-purchase information is lighter than competitors.

## How the 60-Day Cookie Changes the Math

A cookie window is the number of days after a click during which a sale is still credited to you. Sixty days is long for this category.

Why it matters more for written content than for video: someone watching a TikTok demo converts in minutes or not at all. Someone reading a 2,000-word review of an AI video tool is usually mid-research. They read three reviews, open a free account, test it for two weeks, then subscribe — or they subscribe a month later when a client project lands. A 30-day window loses a meaningful share of those. Sixty days keeps most of them.

For contrast, Simplified's affiliate pages state a 30-day window in the body copy and 45 days in the FAQ on the same page — a discrepancy I documented in my [Simplified review](/posts/simplified-review/). Zebracat's single, unambiguous 60 days is a better foundation for a content site.

Do the arithmetic before you get excited about 30% lifetime. Zebracat's own affiliate calculator lists the customer mix as roughly **50% Cat Mode, 40% Super Cat, 10% Unlimited**, and estimates an average plan value near $49/month. At 30%, that is roughly **$15/month per retained customer** — about $176/year. Lifetime sounds enormous; the realistic version is "a customer who stays two years pays you about $350."

## The One Rule That Gets Affiliates Banned

Zebracat's restriction list is short, and one item catches people who otherwise do everything right:

> "No paid ads or discount/promo code sites. Violations lead to suspension."

Two separate prohibitions are packed into that line.

**Paid ads** means you cannot run Google or Meta ads pointing at Zebracat. That includes bidding on the brand name — the FAQ adds "No paid bidding on 'Zebracat' or brand misspellings." So `zebracatt.ai`-style typosquatting campaigns are out.

**Discount and promo-code sites** means a page titled "Zebracat Coupon Code 2026" is a suspension risk, even if it is honest and even if it drives real sales. This is the trap. Coupon pages convert well and are easy to rank, so affiliates build them, and then lose the account.

An editorial review blog is explicitly welcome — "review sites" is named in the accepted-channels list. The distinction the program is drawing is between **publishing an evaluation** and **running a coupon funnel**. If you write reviews, do not drift into promo-code pages for the same brand.
